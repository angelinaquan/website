9 MIT Classes in 1 Semester

← Back to Fun

6.S058, 6.3100, 18.650, 18.619, 5.111, 24.111, 24.118, 24.137, 21T.221.

111 units. That's 111 hours a week, at least on paper, spent in classes and labs, working on problem sets, or studying for exams. Thankfully, there are 168 hours in a week!

On top of that, I was working as an undergraduate researcher, and our paper ended up being published at NeurIPS.

Why?

I wanted to find out how far I could push myself.

If I took five classes every semester and called it a day, I'd never know what I was capable of. I wanted to test that boundary rather than simply assume where it was.

As it turns out, nine classes, with research on the side, was about as far as I could go while staying mentally healthy.

But there was another reason I wanted to take so many classes, and it's one of the things I love most about MIT: I genuinely love learning.

And I did learn a lot that semester, often in subjects I might never have explored otherwise.

  • In philosophy of quantum mechanics, I encountered Everett's interpretation of the measurement problem, which challenged how I thought about what it means to observe something.
  • In control design, I learned how to build and tune controllers for a physical robotic system.
  • In lighting design, I learned to operate stage lights and even got to control the lighting for part of a concert.
  • In principles of chemical science, I explored thermodynamics and acid–base equilibria.

And so much more.

How?

The less glamorous answer is that I was often doing the minimum necessary to understand the material and earn an A.

I skipped as many lectures as I could, learning the material on my own schedule. I took makeup exams because my classes conflicted. I spent weekends in the lab. Much of the semester became an exercise in figuring out how to fit everything in.

And for the most part, it worked.

I learned a tremendous amount, made new friends, and found ways to maintain a reasonably healthy balance. Research, in particular, was something I enjoyed so much that it rarely felt like work.

Still, I sometimes wonder how much more I could have gotten out of those classes if I'd given myself the time to sit with the ideas.

There's a difference between understanding something well enough to solve a problem on an exam and having the time to turn it over in your head, ask your own questions, and explore where those questions lead. I had plenty of opportunities for the former, but not nearly enough for the latter.

And that wasn't the only thing missing.

Looking back, I realize how little time I spent alone with my thoughts. I was almost always in class, working, or spending time with friends. Even my free time tended to fill itself. I was so occupied with doing things that I rarely stopped to reflect on what I was doing, or why.

It's a strange trade-off: I took nine classes because I wanted to make the most of my time at MIT, but in filling my schedule so completely, I may have missed out on a different kind of learning.

I don't regret the semester. I'm glad I tried it, and I'm glad I now have a better sense of my limits.

Would I take nine classes again? Probably. Though knowing what I know now, I'd choose more CS and math classes, the subjects I've become especially interested in.

But I'd also want to leave myself a little more room to think.

After all, finding out how much I can do isn't quite the same as figuring out what I want to do with that capacity.
