Why I Run and the Cambridge Half Marathon

← Back to Fun

I just ran the Cambridge Half Marathon.

A year ago, it took me 13 minutes to run a single mile, and that was me trying my hardest. The idea of running 13.1 miles would have sounded ridiculous.

I started running this summer, when my friend Hannah convinced me to sign up for the JPMorgan Chase 5K.

Before then, I'd never run more than a mile or two. To prepare, we went on a practice run from Mission Bay to Dogpatch, a little over a mile. I remember being slow, tired, and much more interested in the breakfast we had afterwards than in the running itself.

That run didn't exactly make me want to become a runner.

Then came the 5K.

I'd never been part of a race before. There were people everywhere, and when we started, I found myself moving with a crowd of strangers, all running in the same direction. Something about that felt different from running alone.

I tried to keep pace with the people around me and finished the first mile surprisingly quickly.

Then things got harder.

I kept repeating the same words to myself: Keep going. Keep going. Keep going.

I wanted to walk, but everyone around me was still running. High school volunteers stood along the course, cheering and offering high-fives. I told myself that, at the very least, I wanted to run the whole thing.

Somewhere in the second half of the race, I noticed something strange.

I'd spent much of the summer feeling anxious and uncertain about my future. There was always something in the back of my mind to worry about, even when I had nothing immediate to do.

But as I ran, those thoughts began to disappear. I was too occupied with my breathing, my legs, and the simple task of getting to the finish line to think about much else.

During the last mile, I started to cry a little.

I wasn't sad. If anything, I felt relieved. For the first time in a while, I wasn't thinking about what came next. All I had to do was keep running.

Maybe that was my first runner's high.

When I crossed the finish line, I'd run farther than I ever had before. I was slow, exhausted, and disproportionately proud of myself.

And I wanted to do it again.

So I started running on my own.

When I felt anxious, I ran. When I felt good, I ran. Sometimes I ran for no particular reason.

Eventually, Hannah suggested we run a half marathon.

Thirteen miles still sounded absurd, but now I was curious.

I started with six miles, just to see if I could finish without stopping. Then I worked on running three miles a little faster. Then eight miles. Then more three-mile runs.

Progress was gradual, but it was also unmistakable. Distances that once seemed impossible became manageable. I could run farther, recover faster, and occasionally surprise myself.

I also got used to being bad at something.

I've spent a lot of my life doing things I'm reasonably good at, or at least things where I know how to get better. Running was different. There were always people passing me, people who could run twice as far or twice as fast.

At first, I was conscious of how slow I was. Eventually, I became less interested in comparing myself to other runners. I had my own previous times and distances to measure myself against, and that was enough.

There's something satisfying about being a beginner and having no particular expectation of being good.

Then came the Cambridge Half Marathon.

I got up, went to the starting line, and ran 13.1 miles.

My legs were exhausted afterwards. But a distance that would have been unthinkable a year ago had become something I could simply wake up one morning and do.

I don't think I've become a particularly good runner.

But I've become someone who runs.

And I want to keep going.
