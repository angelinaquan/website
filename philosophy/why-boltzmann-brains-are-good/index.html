Why Boltzmann Brains Are Good

Angelina Quan · Fall 2026
BOLTMANN COSMOLOGY

In “Why Boltzmann Brains Are Bad,” Sean Carroll argues that cosmological theories dominated by Boltzmann brains should be rejected because they are cognitively unstable. A Boltzmann brain is a conscious observer that forms through a random fluctuation rather than through an ordinary causal history involving evolution, perception, and memory. Some cosmological theories imply that, over the full history of the universe, Boltzmann brains would vastly outnumber ordinary observers. More troublingly, many of these Boltzmann brains could be exact or near-exact duplicates of us: they could have experiences, apparent memories, and apparent evidence that are phenomenally indistinguishable from ours. If that is right, then it seems natural to wonder whether we ourselves are Boltzmann brains.

Carroll thinks that this possibility creates a fatal problem for any theory that predicts Boltzmann-brain domination. If a theory implies that I am probably a Boltzmann brain, then it also implies that my apparent memories and observations are probably the products of random fluctuation. But those memories and observations include the scientific evidence that led me to believe the cosmological theory in the first place. The theory therefore seems to undermine the very evidence on which belief in it depends. Carroll concludes that such theories cannot simultaneously be true and justifiably believed.

I agree with Carroll that a Boltzmann-brain-dominated cosmology would have radical skeptical consequences. But I do not think those consequences show that the cosmology should be rejected. If strong scientific evidence supports a theory according to which observers phenomenally indistinguishable from me are overwhelmingly Boltzmann brains, and if a plausible principle of self-locating indifference is correct, then I should become highly confident that I am a Boltzmann brain. The fact that this conclusion subsequently undermines some of my confidence in my apparent past does not make the conclusion irrational. Carroll treats cognitive instability as evidence that something has gone wrong with the theory. I argue that it may instead be something we have learned about our own epistemic position.

The first step is to distinguish two questions. One is whether the universe is such that Boltzmann brains vastly outnumber ordinary observers. The other is whether I myself am a Boltzmann brain. These are not the same question. Suppose, however, that excellent scientific evidence strongly supports a cosmological theory $T$ according to which Boltzmann brains dominate. Suppose further that $T$ predicts not merely many strange observers, but vast numbers of observers whose present experiences are phenomenally indistinguishable from mine.

Imagine that $T$ predicts one ordinary observer having my present experience for every $10^{100}$ Boltzmann brains having exactly the same experience. The ordinary observer remembers growing up, going to school, reading philosophy, and encountering the cosmological evidence for $T$. Each Boltzmann brain has apparent memories with exactly the same phenomenal character, even though those memories do not correspond to a genuine past.

At that point, a natural principle of self-locating reasoning becomes relevant. John Pittard calls the relevant idea Center Indifference. Roughly, if several observers are indistinguishable with respect to my evidence, I should not arbitrarily assign much greater probability to being one rather than another. If one observer compatible with my present evidence is ordinary while $10^{100}$ are Boltzmann brains, then conditional on $T$, I should assign overwhelming probability to being a Boltzmann brain.

Formally, let $B$ be the proposition that I am a Boltzmann brain, $O$ the proposition that I am an ordinary observer, and $E$ my total present phenomenal evidence. If Boltzmann brains and ordinary observers can instantiate exactly the same experiential state, then my present experience does not discriminate between them:

\[ P(E\mid B) \approx P(E\mid O). \]

If there are vastly more $B$-centers than $O$-centers compatible with $E$, then Center Indifference strongly favors $B$. In the extreme case,

\[ P(B\mid T,E)\approx 1. \]

The important point is that I cannot appeal to the vividness, coherence, or apparent continuity of my experience as evidence that I am ordinary. A Boltzmann brain duplicate would have the same apparent coherence. It could seem to remember yesterday, childhood, scientific experiments, and the argument in the previous paragraph. From the inside, there need be no phenomenal difference.

This is where the Boltzmann-brain problem becomes genuinely epistemological. Once we accept a theory that predicts overwhelming numbers of observers exactly like us, maintaining confidence that we are among the tiny minority of ordinary observers requires explanation. Simply insisting that “of course I am the normal one” looks like an unjustified privilege assigned to one’s own center.

Carroll nevertheless argues that we should reject theories that lead us here. His central concern is cognitive instability. Suppose I believe $T$ because of astronomical observations, memories of experiments, testimony from scientists, and so on. Then $T$ tells me that I am probably a Boltzmann brain. But if I am a Boltzmann brain, those apparent observations and memories were probably randomly generated. I therefore lose my justification for trusting them. Since those are the very things that justified $T$, it appears that belief in $T$ destroys its own evidential basis.

This argument is initially powerful. But it moves too quickly from “this belief undermines some of its own evidential sources” to “this belief is therefore irrational.” Adam Elga’s discussion of cognitive instability helps reveal the gap. There are cases in which evidence rationally leads us to discover that the source of that evidence is unreliable. The resulting state can look paradoxical without actually being incoherent.

Consider a simple example. Suppose I inspect a device that appears to provide strong evidence that the device itself has recently begun malfunctioning. After learning this, I should become less confident in future outputs from the device, and perhaps less confident in some of its earlier outputs as well. But it does not follow that I must immediately erase the information that led me to discover the malfunction and return to my prior beliefs. Evidence can have a rational effect even when its content later gives us reason to downgrade the reliability of its source.

The Boltzmann-brain case is more extreme, but the structure is similar. My apparent scientific evidence may rationally lead me toward $T$. The combination of $T$ and Center Indifference may then rationally lead me toward $B$. Learning $B$, in turn, may rationally force me to reduce confidence in many propositions that depend on the reliability of my apparent memories and perceptions. None of this yet establishes that I was irrational to move toward $B$.

Carroll’s argument therefore seems to rely on an additional principle: if accepting a theory would undermine one’s trust in the evidence supporting that theory, then the theory cannot be rationally believed. But that principle is too strong. Reality is not required to be epistemically friendly. It is possible, at least in principle, for a rational agent to acquire evidence that she occupies a deeply unfavorable epistemic situation.

Suppose a trustworthy doctor gives me overwhelming evidence that a drug I have taken causes severe memory distortion. I may rationally conclude that many of my recent memories are unreliable. That conclusion is epistemically unpleasant, but its unpleasantness does not count as evidence against the doctor’s claim. More generally, discovering that I am cognitively compromised would be bad news about me, not automatically evidence that the discovery itself is false.

The same distinction matters in the Boltzmann-brain case. Carroll treats the skeptical consequences of $T$ as a reductio of $T$. But another response is available: accept the skeptical consequences.

This does not mean that becoming confident that I am a Boltzmann brain should destroy all of my beliefs. Different beliefs depend on different kinds of justification. If I become highly confident that my apparent causal history is fabricated, I should reduce my confidence in propositions such as “I ate breakfast yesterday,” “this experiment actually occurred,” or “I learned this fact from a reliable textbook.” These beliefs depend heavily on memory and causal interaction with an external world.

But some beliefs may survive much better. My confidence that I am currently having a certain experience need not depend on whether I am an ordinary observer. Nor does a mathematical truth such as $2+2=4$ become false because my apparent childhood is fabricated. Conditional reasoning may also remain intact. Even if I am uncertain whether the evidence I seem to remember was genuinely gathered, I can still reason about what would follow if certain premises were true.

So the conclusion “I am probably a Boltzmann brain” need not collapse into total irrationality. It may instead force a selective revision of beliefs according to their dependence on unreliable sources.

Pittard sharpens the skeptical challenge further. He argues that the deepest problem may not be simply the possibility that I am a Boltzmann brain. The more serious possibility is that my appearances concerning the scientific evidence are themselves radically misleading. If a theory such as SUPERABUNDANCE would undermine my trust in my evidence, then perhaps I should think that I have somehow been misled about what the science supports.

But this raises the same question in a new form: why should I prefer the hypothesis that my apparent evidence is misleading over the hypothesis that I am a Boltzmann brain? If the scientific case for Boltzmann-brain domination appears extremely strong, there are several possible reactions. I might reject the cosmology. I might reject Center Indifference. I might deny that Boltzmann brains can be conscious. Or I might accept that I am probably one of them.

The last option sounds absurd, but absurdity is not an argument. If the other premises are independently well supported, then refusing to accept their consequence merely because it is skeptical risks building a bias against skeptical conclusions into our theory of rationality.

This is why, in a limited sense, Boltzmann brains are “good.” They are not good because being a Boltzmann brain would be desirable. They are good because they expose a temptation in epistemology: the temptation to treat any theory that undermines our ordinary epistemic confidence as automatically unacceptable. Boltzmann brains force us to separate two questions that are easy to conflate: whether a theory is epistemically disturbing, and whether the theory is false.

A universe dominated by Boltzmann brains would be profoundly epistemically hostile. It would make our apparent histories unreliable and radically reduce our confidence in many ordinary beliefs. But it does not follow that such a universe is impossible, nor that evidence could never rationally lead us to believe we inhabit one.

If our best-supported cosmology implied that almost all observers phenomenally indistinguishable from us were Boltzmann brains, and if Center Indifference is correct, then the rational response may be to accept that we are probably Boltzmann brains. Carroll interprets the resulting cognitive instability as a defect in the cosmological theory. We should instead take seriously the possibility that it is a discovery about ourselves.
